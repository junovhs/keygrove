# Movement vocabulary — research checkpoint

**Status: research checkpoint / provisional.** Nothing in this document is app
code or a lesson order. It synthesises the candidate sets generated by RES-01
(cross-corpus stability) and RES-03 (candidate sets) as they stand on
2026-09-22. Sources: `outputs/cross-corpus-stability.md`,
`outputs/candidates.md`, `outputs/candidates/*.csv`. No new analysis or
scoring was done to write it.

## 1. What the candidate sets are

Four CSVs under `outputs/candidates/`, all derived from within-word letter
n-grams in three corpora (Google Books, Norvig web, SUBTLEX-US subtitles),
classified under the Traditional QWERTY finger table.

| set | size | one-line description |
|---|---|---|
| **core transitions** | 43 bigrams | The bigrams that are top-60 in *every* corpus: `th he in er an re on at en nd ti es or te of ed is it al ar st to nt ng se ha as ou le ve co me hi ri ne ea li ll om ur ca el ho` |
| **technical transitions** | 40 bigrams | Cross-corpus-stable bigrams that also have a named mechanical reason. 25 are same-finger; 15 are row jumps, stretches or pinky reaches. |
| **gestures** | 30 trigrams + 10 4-grams | Stable chunks with a named shape: redirect, roll, same-finger inside, or two row changes. |
| **practice words** | 914 words / 80 targets | For each technical transition and gesture, up to 12 everyday words that contain it. |

## 2. What membership actually means

**Core** = "a learner will meet this constantly, in every register of
English." It is a coverage list. Membership says nothing about difficulty and
does not imply a drill; it implies *a course that never generates this bigram
has a hole.*

**Technical** = two conditions, both required:

- *Frequency:* stable across all three corpora. For same-finger transitions
  the comparison is against other same-finger transitions (top-30 of that
  sub-list), not against all bigrams — because `mu` will never out-rank `th`,
  and that comparison isn't the relevant one.
- *Mechanics:* one of four descriptive reasons — same finger (25 rows),
  same-hand top↔bottom row jump (20), stretch to `y`/`b` (7), pinky off home
  or ring on bottom row (2). Reasons overlap; a row carries all that apply.

Membership means "common everywhere *and* mechanically distinct enough to
name." It does not mean "hard." Nothing has been scored.

**Gestures** = stable trigram/4-gram with a shape feature. Note the
asymmetry: 20 of 30 trigrams and 9 of 10 4-grams are there on
`double_row_change` alone, which 45% of all trigram mass has. That feature is
weak as a filter; it mostly lets frequency decide. The 4-grams are
"two-corpus" (no web 4-gram table exists) — a stated, weaker standard.

**Practice words** = SUBTLEX-frequent (≥ ~5 per million), appearing in ≥ 100
films, mostly lower-case in use (drops names), not a contraction stem or
swear word, and present in the Google Books list. Ordered so short carriers
come first (`mud, much, must…` before `community`). Membership means "an
everyday word that contains the target," not "a good drill word."

## 3. Where the sets overlap

- **Core ∩ technical = 5 bigrams**: `in on ed ve om`. Four of those
  (`in on ve om`) entered technical via the row-jump reason, and they are
  among the most frequent bigrams in English. `ed` is the one same-finger
  transition high enough to be core.
- **Technical → gestures**: the same-finger core (`ed`, `ce`, `tr`, `rt`)
  reappears inside gestures `ted ide nce art` (`contains_same_finger`). The
  gesture set is largely the technical set embedded in a third letter.
- **Gestures ↔ 4-grams**: `ion`/`tion`, `the`/`ther`/`othe`, `thi`/`this`,
  `ing`/`ting`, `eve`/`ever` are the same movement at two lengths. The 4-gram
  list adds little that the trigram list doesn't already say.
- **Practice words** overlap heavily across targets: `something`,
  `everything`, `interested`, `started`, `understand` each serve 3–5 targets.
  That's a feature for later (one word can exercise several targets) but it
  means "914 words" overstates the vocabulary — the distinct-word count is
  much smaller.

## 4. What looks important or surprising

**The same-finger list is remarkably stable.** 25 of the Google Books top-30
same-finger transitions are universal, and the top nine are in near-identical
order in all three corpora: `ed de ce ec tr un lo ol rt`. This is the
strongest single result. Whatever else changes, these nine are real.

**`ed` alone is 17% of all same-finger movement**, and the L-middle finger
(`ed de ce ec`) carries about 44% of it. The R-index (`un um mu nu ny my ju
hu`) and L-index (`tr rt fr gr br rg ft rf rv`) carry most of the rest.
Same-finger practice is essentially a three-finger problem.

**Book artefacts are not a bigram problem.** Zero Google Books top-200
bigrams fall out of the top-300 elsewhere. At bigram level, "which corpus"
barely matters. At trigram level it does: 62 of the books top-200 trigrams
drop in one of the other corpora, and they split cleanly into Latinate
written-English chunks (`cti ica iti tic ity enc`) that conversational
English lacks, and function-word chunks (`whi wer oul but uld she hey`) the
web corpus under-counts.

**The row-jump reason floods.** Without the same-finger-first rule,
`in on ve om no be mo mi ni im` would have pushed `mu`, `um`, `ny`, `ft` out
of the 40-cap on mass alone. That the cap needed a priority rule is a signal
that "same-hand row jump" and "same finger" are not the same kind of reason.

**Conversational English is less same-finger-heavy** (5.8% vs 6.9% books,
7.7% web) and has fewer double same-finger chains (0.28% vs 0.50%).
Subtitles are shorter, more Germanic words. `ded`, `olo`, `ece` — the
double-chain examples from the gesture atlas — all fall out of SUBTLEX's
top-500.

**`my` and `yo` are the biggest register swings** among stable items: `my` is
same-finger rank 6 in subtitles but 27 on the web; `yo` is bigram rank 7 in
subtitles, 157 in books. Second-person conversational English is what a
learner types in chat and email, and it's under-represented in books.

## 5. What is still assumption, not evidence

1. **Every threshold.** N=200/500/30, core rank ≤ 60, cap 40, stretch ≥ 1.5
   key widths, word floor 250, CD ≥ 100, lower-case ≥ 50%. All chosen by
   judgment; none validated. Moving any of them changes set edges, not cores.
2. **The four mechanical reasons.** "Same finger" is well-motivated by
   DEC-13. "Same-hand row jump," "stretch to y/b," and the specific definition
   of "weak-finger reach" (pinky off home, ring on bottom — *not* `o` or `w`)
   are plausible descriptions with no learner data behind them.
3. **That mechanical reasons predict learner difficulty at all.** DEC-16
   forbids a difficulty score for exactly this reason. Until error/hesitation
   data exists, "technical" is a hypothesis about what will be hard.
4. **Same-finger sub-ranking as admission evidence.** This is the rule that
   lets `mu` in. It is defensible but it is a choice, and 13 of the 40
   technical rows depend on it.
5. **Word-list derivation for SUBTLEX.** Its n-grams come from a word list
   weighted by frequency, not running text, and contractions arrive split
   (`don` + `t`). The Google Books tables were built the same way, so the
   comparison is fair, but neither is running text.
6. **Practice-word quality.** The filters remove names and profanity; they do
   not ensure a word is a *good carrier* (short, common, target in a natural
   position). `mud` first for `mu` is what concentration-sort gives; whether
   it's what a lesson wants is untested.
7. **4-gram stability** rests on two corpora.
8. **Traditional table as the selection basis** (DEC-12). Under Relaxed,
   `ce ec br be ab` change class — `ce`/`ec` stop being same-finger entirely.
   A Relaxed learner's technical set is not this set.

## 6. Smallest plausible curriculum interpretation

Read the sets as three tiers, nothing more:

- **43 core bigrams: coverage, not drills.** The existing word/phrase
  generators almost certainly already produce these; the only action is a
  coverage check that they do.
- **11 technical targets for v1: `ed de ce ec tr un lo ol rt mu um`.** The
  nine same-finger transitions that are stable and ordered everywhere, plus
  the R-index pair `mu/um` that DEC-13 uses as its example. Eleven targets on
  three fingers, each with 8–12 everyday carrier words ready. This is the
  entire "technical" curriculum a first version needs.
- **A few obvious chunks: `ing`, `ion/tion`, `nce`, `ted`.** Already the most
  common chunks in the language; treat them as words, not gestures — the
  gesture framing adds a label to something learners will over-practise
  anyway.
- **Everything else stays provisional until learner data justifies promoting
  it** — the other 14 same-finger transitions, the row-jumps, stretches,
  rolls, 4-grams, and the 60-odd extra gestures remain in the CSVs, waiting
  for error/hesitation data to say whether any of it is actually hard.
