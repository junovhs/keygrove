# KeyGrove Research Handoff — 2026-09-21

## Current state

The project is investigating a typing curriculum based on **motor learning**, especially recurring transitions and short gestures.

The central analogy is classical instrumental practice:

> isolate a difficult movement, practice it deliberately, then put it back into meaningful material.

The desired product is deliberately much simpler than the research underneath it.

## Product north star

**Small surface. Deep model.**

A learner should be able to open the app, do a useful 2–5 minute lesson, and leave.

The curriculum engine can be sophisticated. The interface should not expose that complexity unnecessarily.

Do not turn this into a large educational management system.

## Core pedagogical idea

Typing fluency is not merely knowledge of key locations.

A useful hierarchy is:

**key → transition → gesture → word → real language**

Examples:

- `m` and `u` may both be known while `mu` remains awkward.
- `mu` can be isolated.
- It can then appear in `must`, `much`, `music`, `community`.
- Finally it appears in ordinary prose.

The same principle extends to trigrams and short gestures.

## Speed

Current belief:

**Speed is mostly an emergent result, not the primary training objective.**

Accuracy, control, relaxation, useful repetition, familiarity, and automaticity should produce speed.

WPM may still be measured.

Avoid making constant speed pressure the center of the experience.

## Core practice forms

Only three special forms have been selected so far:

### Transition loops

Repeat a target movement or gesture cleanly.

### Steady beat / rhythm

Type a sequence to a regular pulse to practice control and consistent timing.

### Words / etudes

Use concentrated real words built around a technical target.

Ordinary prose provides transfer practice.

Do not create a giant menu of training modes without evidence that it is needed.

## Fingering limitation

A conventional keyboard reports the key pressed, not the physical finger used.

Therefore the app can:

- teach standard fingering
- show the intended finger
- remind learners about technique
- notice suspicious timing/error patterns

But it cannot truthfully claim to know that the correct finger was used.

Do not build the core product around extra sensing hardware.

### Baseline frequency work

From Norvig's Google Books-derived data:

- 669 observed bigrams
- 8,653 observed trigrams
- 42,171 observed 4-grams

Coverage:

- 41 bigrams → 50%
- 97 → 75%
- 168 → 90%
- 214 → 95%
- 300 → 99%

The top 200 bigrams represent about **93.8%** of within-word bigram occurrence mass.

### Bigram movement findings

Under conventional US QWERTY fingering:

- alternate hands: ~52.87%
- same hand / different fingers: ~37.69%
- same finger / different key: ~6.94%
- repeated key: ~2.51%

High-frequency same-finger transitions include:

`ed de ce ec tr un lo ol rt fr gr um mu`

Important example:

`mu` is uncommon relative to the most frequent bigrams but still meaningful and recurring.

`qa` is mechanically unusual but vastly rarer, illustrating why mechanical awkwardness alone should not determine curriculum priority.

### Trigram findings

About **13.53%** of trigram occurrence mass contains at least one adjacent same-finger transition.

The trigram analysis also identifies:

- same-finger chains
- same-finger reversals
- separated finger reuse
- inward rolls
- outward rolls
- redirects
- alternating hands
- repeated row changes

Examples:

- `ion` is an extremely common same-hand redirect.
- `ing` is extremely common and changes rows twice.
- patterns such as `ded`, `olo`, `ece`, `num`, and `mun` show concentrated same-finger gesture behavior.

These are descriptive motor categories, not proven difficulty scores.

## Important correction made during research

The first geometry model incorrectly treated the physical distance between two keys pressed by different fingers as though one finger traveled that distance.

That was corrected.

The current model distinguishes:

- reach of each finger from its home key
- actual key-to-key travel only for same-finger transitions

Do not use the original `movement-atlas.md` distance interpretation as a difficulty measure.

Prefer `movement-atlas-v2.md`.

## What research should do next

Do not keep expanding the taxonomy for its own sake.

The highest-value next validation is **cross-corpus stability**:

Compare Google Books results against:

- SUBTLEX-US
- Norvig web corpus

Questions:

- Which transitions stay important everywhere?
- Which are mostly artifacts of book language?
- Which gestures look universal enough for a core curriculum?

After that, produce candidate sets rather than endless analysis:

- likely core transitions
- likely technical transitions
- likely useful gestures
- natural words for each

These should remain provisional until real learner data exists.

## Guiding constraints for future work

Prefer:

- simple defaults
- short lessons
- direct practice
- meaningful language
- invisible adaptation
- empirically useful curriculum decisions

Be suspicious of:

- giant progression trees
- dozens of modes
- currencies and reward systems
- dashboards
- excessive settings
- fake precision
- premature scoring formulas
- features that create maintenance work without improving practice

## The actual thing being built

Not:

> a comprehensive science platform for typing

Not:

> a gamified productivity ecosystem

Not:

> a giant curriculum authoring system

It is:

> **a small, excellent typing teacher that understands movement unusually well.**
